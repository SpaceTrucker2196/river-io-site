Welfare & Habitat Standards

The Feline Passenger Standard, the transfer decks, and the Board that can close any facility in the network. Feline welfare is not a program at United Cat Foods. It is load-bearing infrastructure, budgeted accordingly.

Every live cat moving through the network travels under the Feline Passenger Standard, a document with the legal standing of a pressure-hull certification and roughly the same room for interpretation. A cat aboard a U.C.F. vessel is a passenger. The word is doing regulatory work: passengers have manifests, itineraries, veterinary records, and named responsible officers. Cargo has none of those things, which is why a cat is never cargo.

The Standard covers clearance, carriage, housing, interaction service, and retirement. Compliance is audited by inspectors who arrive unannounced, hold master keys, and are famously unbribable with treats, because the treats are inventoried too.

Passengers, never cargo

The physical expression of the Standard is the transfer deck. Every station U.C.F. serves maintains two separated environments at the dock: a freight deck, where tanks and pods and pallets move on loader schedules, and a feline transfer corridor, pressurised, climate-held, and acoustically isolated behind its own bulkhead. The two do not mix. A habitat module never crosses the freight deck; a pallet never enters the corridor.

Aboard ship, the same rule holds. Loaf-class vessels carry passenger suites amidships, at the node of least acceleration noise, with independent life support and their own water loop. The suites are inspected before boarding, after landing, and at any moment an inspector feels like it.

  • Individual habitats. One passenger per module, sized to stand, turn, stretch full length, and ignore the viewport with dignity.
  • Climate hold. Temperature and humidity within feline comfort band at all times, including during dock transfer, including during alarms, including during drills.
  • Escort ratio. A certified live-cat escort per six passengers, trained in handling, first response, and the correct way to lose gracefully at staring contests.
  • Quiet transit. No passenger corridor shares a wall with drive machinery. The Standard specifies maximum decibels; the cats specify lower ones.

Habitat minimums

Fixed facilities, from station cafés to the L5 gardens, meet the same schedule of minimums. The schedule is public, because publishing it was cheaper than answering questions about it.

  • Vertical territory. Elevated routes and perches on at least two levels, reachable without crossing human traffic. High ground is not an amenity. It is a right.
  • Protected retreat. Enclosed spaces a human cannot follow into, marked by architecture rather than signage, honoured by staff without exception.
  • Quiet-sleep habitat. Dedicated dark, warm, low-traffic sleeping volume per resident, held to a stricter noise budget than the bridge of a Loaf-class.
  • Scratch infrastructure. Rated surfaces at regulation angles, replaced on wear schedule, not after complaint.
  • Water quality. Café and habitat water is held to the feline renal standard, which is stricter than the human potable standard. Humans drink it and report feeling vaguely honoured.
  • Sanitation separation. Litter service is engineered, ventilated, and never adjacent to food service. This clause has never needed enforcement. Everyone involved is a professional.

Interaction service

Human interaction is a service cats provide, on terms the Standard makes explicit. The operating principle is voluntary approach: visitors sit, cats decide. Staff exist to maintain the conditions under which deciding is pleasant.

Capacity is not seating. A café's rated capacity is its interaction availability: resident count, times available hours, times a wellbeing coefficient the Board audits from habitat telemetry. When utilisation holds above the Board's recommended 71 percent threshold, the schedule thins, prices rise, and the cats nap. Corporate has learned to describe this as yield management.

  • Rest rotas. Mandatory off-floor rest per shift, logged, with the log audited against the telemetry rather than against the manager's memory.
  • Session supervision. Trained staff on the floor at all times; visitor briefing before first entry; children accompanied and outnumbered.
  • Health screening. Veterinary clearance per passenger per posting, temperament screening per venue, and a standing rule that any cat may retire from service at any time by the traditional method of simply not showing up.
  • Telemetry. Habitat conditions streamed to the Board continuously. The dashboard is the same one the cats would build, if they cared to prove what they already know.

Enforcement

The Feline Welfare Board holds authority U.C.F. corporate does not: it can close any interaction facility in the network, ground any passenger service, and has done both. The Neko Station closure of 3021 lasted eleven days, cost a quarter's regional margin, and ended when the quiet-sleep habitat was rebuilt three square meters larger. The Board's final report ran to one sentence: "Adequate."

Corporate accepts this arrangement because the alternative is worse, and because the Board is right. A network that moves live passengers on the strength of its word keeps its word where the passengers sleep. The margins recovered. The habitat stayed.
