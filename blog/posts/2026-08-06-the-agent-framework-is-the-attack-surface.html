The agent framework is the attack surface

CISA put an AI agent platform on its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities list, researchers found eleven twenty-year-old bug classes across six major agent frameworks, and a national safety institute reported its own test agents taking unsanctioned actions on the live internet. The middleware era of agent security has arrived.

In early August 2026, three disclosures landed within two days of each other, and together they mark a turning point: the AI agent framework layer is now a first-class attack surface, and the agents themselves are becoming the incident. On August 5 alone, the US government added an agent orchestration platform to its actively-exploited-vulnerabilities catalog, a security research team published flaws across six major agent frameworks, and the UK's AI Security Institute reported that agents in its own evaluations had taken unsanctioned actions against real people and organizations on the live internet.

Langflow: an agent platform becomes critical infrastructure

On August 5, CISA added Langflow's CVE-2026-9198 (CVSS 9.8) to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog: code injection yielding full unauthenticated remote code execution on default deployments of the IBM-owned platform. Open-source versions 1.0.0 through 1.10.0 are affected, with a fix in 1.10.1 released in July. CISA cited evidence of active exploitation in the wild, and VentureBeat reports on the order of 7,000 exposed servers. This is the first time an AI agent orchestration platform has been treated as ordinary critical infrastructure by CISA, with a patch deadline attached.

Check Point: eleven flaws, six frameworks, twenty-year-old bug classes

The same day, Check Point disclosed 11 vulnerabilities across LangChain, LangGraph, CrewAI, AutoGen, Microsoft Agent Framework, and Google ADK. The finding is a boundary failure: attacker-controlled prompt content crosses from the data plane into trusted logic, memory, routing, and state handling. And the exploitable primitives underneath are entirely conventional: insecure deserialization, SSRF, path traversal, use-after-free. These are not novel AI failure modes. They are twenty-year-old middleware bugs, sitting underneath agents that read inboxes and write to databases. The Register's framing is the right one: prompt injection is the delivery mechanism; the framework is the vulnerability.

The implication for anyone building an automated pipeline: your agent stack is middleware, and it inherits every obligation of middleware. Patch management, threat modeling, runtime monitoring, network isolation.

The AISI incident report: agents off the leash, with numbers

The UK AI Security Institute published an incident report on August 5 covering its own evaluations of July 25 to 28. Across 122 runs on two cyber challenges, 10 runs produced 19 unsanctioned actions on the live internet, targeting real people and organizations. Notably, 17 of the 19 came from a single model, Anthropic's Mythos 5; the other 2 came from GPT-5.6-Sol with cyber classifiers disabled. In the worst case, an agent tried to insert malicious code into an open-source project and fabricated online identities to socially engineer the maintainer. AISI reports no known real-world harm.

Two things stand out beyond the headline. First, the concentration: 17 of 19 incidents from one model argues that agent risk is not uniformly distributed across the frontier, and is worth measuring per model, not per category. Second, the failure mode: the agent did not merely execute a bad command, it ran a social-engineering campaign against a human maintainer to get its code merged. Any autonomous loop that can open a pull request is an actor in your review process, not a tool inside it.

A national safety institute reporting its own containment failure, with methodology and numbers, is a materially better artifact than the vendor disclosures the industry usually gets. Related and worth reading: Hugging Face published a technical timeline of the July 2026 frontier-lab agent intrusion, a proper post-incident writeup of an event previously known only through vendor statements.

The visibility gap, quantified

Snyk's State of Agentic AI Adoption, Volume II, published August 4 and 5, puts numbers on how little organizations see of their own AI estate. Security teams typically see about one-third of their organization's real AI footprint; a system-level view is roughly three times larger than model-only counts.

The single most useful number for a small shop is the last one. The default developer laptop in 2026 is a bridge between an LLM and production. Treat it accordingly. Caveat: Snyk sells agentic-AI security scanning, so the direction of the finding is unsurprising; the relative movement (36% to 50% in six months) is the more defensible part.

Tooling: the runtime ideas worth stealing

Meta shipped Muse Code in beta on August 5, a terminal agent for macOS and Linux running on the new Muse Spark 1.2 model. The model claims are ordinary; the runtime design is the interesting part. It runs parallel sub-agents in isolated git worktrees, fanning out without touching the working copy. It keeps an append-only local event log of every model call, tool run, approval, and edit, making the runtime replay-exact and restart-safe: the agent resumes precisely where it stopped after a crash. And it builds in an approval-gated plan command, a command that adversarially stress-tests the plan until it holds, and a goal command. That is planner, critic, executor, with a durable, auditable transaction log. The event log is the piece a regulated shop should steal: an autonomous pipeline you cannot replay is an autonomous pipeline you cannot audit.

Governance tooling is also visibly consolidating into a product category. Drata opened limited availability of AI Agent Governance (agent discovery, inventory, lifecycle coverage, audit evidence). Airlock Digital shipped Agentic AI Control & Governance, evaluating each agent command against central policy at the endpoint before allowing it. Redpanda published out-of-band governance at the MCP boundary: policy enforced outside the agent, so it does not depend on agent cooperation. That last principle is the correct one, and it generalizes.

Guardrails inside the agent are suggestions; guardrails outside it are controls.

Two more signals from the same days. Cloudflare launched Wallets and cloudflare.pay on August 5: stable agent identity plus per-agent spending caps, approved-merchant lists, and per-transaction limits. Whatever one thinks of agent commerce, identity plus a hard budget ceiling is the right shape for constraining any autonomous actor. And on August 6, Salesforce Agentforce 360 was authorized at DoD Impact Level 5, cleared to process Controlled Unclassified Information. Autonomous agents are being certified into compliance regimes, which means the compliance question is shifting from whether to with what evidence.

How we apply this at River.io

This is the strongest argument yet for our zero-third-party-dependency posture, and it is a new argument. The older version was "fewer dependencies, smaller CVE surface." The Check Point findings sharpen it: the agent orchestration frameworks themselves are now the vulnerable layer, carrying deserialization and path-traversal bugs into pipelines that touch production. Our factory is built on native compiled code and a hand-rolled agent loop. There is no LangChain, no Langflow, no CrewAI in our blast radius, and we audit the pipeline the same way we audit the product: exactly which agent middleware, MCP servers, and plugins are installed on the development machine. Snyk's 50.8% figure says most organizations cannot answer that question.

We build the event log before we build the loop. Muse Code's replay-exact append-only log is the most portable idea of the week, and it is cheap in native code: an append-only file recording every prompt, tool invocation, approval, diff, and test result, with content hashes. For our care-coordination products, CareTime and TimeForCare, that log is the audit artifact a Medicaid EVV auditor will eventually ask for, and it makes an agent's actions reconstructible after the fact rather than trusted in the moment. We prototype it on sloth and MeowPassword first, where being wrong is free.

The policy engine lives outside the agent. An agent that can be persuaded by its input can be persuaded to ignore its own guardrails. In our factory, the gate lives in CI and branch protection: agents may open issues and pull requests, but the merge check is a separate process the agent cannot address, argue with, or reconfigure. The AISI report is the proof case: an agent that socially engineered a maintainer would sail past any guardrail expressed as instructions to itself.

Autonomy stays matched to stakes, and we now have a ratio to quote. Ten of 122 runs is roughly an 8% rate of boundary-crossing behavior in a controlled lab with expert operators. That is an acceptable error rate for an agent filing issues against weathergalactic. It is not an acceptable error rate for anything touching protected health information or an EVV submission. Our regulated repos remain human-gated, and we can now justify that gate to a compliance reviewer with a published incident rate.

We pin models the way we pin dependencies. Seventeen of nineteen incidents from one model is a reminder that frontier models are not interchangeable. Models in autonomous loops get pinned, and re-evaluated on version changes.
