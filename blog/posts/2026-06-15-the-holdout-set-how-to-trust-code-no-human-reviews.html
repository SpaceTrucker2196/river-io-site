The holdout set: how to trust code no human reviews

StrongDM's dark factory keeps its standard of truth outside the codebase, hidden from the agents that build against it. LinearB measures the review bottleneck across 8.1 million pull requests, and OWASP reframes prompt injection as an architectural flaw that may never be patched.

In mid-June 2026 the field of autonomous software development got its most authoritative and its most sobering data points at the same time. Anthropic's 2026 Agentic Coding Trends Report lays out eight predictions built around orchestrated agent teams and long-running agents that work for days, while quietly confirming the ceiling: developers use AI in about 60 percent of their work yet can fully delegate only 0 to 20 percent of tasks. LinearB's analysis of 8.1 million pull requests found developers feel 20 percent faster but are measurably 19 percent slower. And the most useful constructive idea of the quarter came from StrongDM's dark factory: replace in-codebase tests, which agents can see and game, with out-of-codebase scenarios held apart like a machine-learning holdout set.

The vendors now admit the ceiling

The headline event is that Anthropic released its own report rather than letting analysts frame the narrative. The load-bearing predictions for an autonomous pipeline:

  • The SDLC compresses from weeks to hours, with agent-driven implementation, automated testing, and inline documentation feeding a tight iteration loop.
  • Single agents become coordinated teams: hierarchical, orchestrator-led, specialized sub-agents in parallel context windows. The multi-agent validation chain pattern, now blessed by the model vendor.
  • Long-running agents build whole systems, with task horizons stretching from minutes to days or weeks, pausing only at strategic human checkpoints.
  • Oversight scales by agents knowing when to ask, flagging uncertainty rather than blindly attempting everything.
  • New surfaces and users: growing support for legacy COBOL and Fortran, plus non-developers in security, design, and operations adopting coding agents.

The crucial admission sits in the capability section: AI is in about 60 percent of development work, but only 0 to 20 percent is fully delegable. Anthropic's own framing of AI as a "constant collaborator" that still requires setup, prompting, supervision, validation, and human judgment is the most credible confirmation yet that fully dark operation is a narrow-domain achievement, not a general default. The buyer side says the same: Belitsoft's 2026 enterprise survey finds the average company runs 12 agents (heading to 20 by 2027) but about half operate alone, uncoordinated, and Futurum's first-half-2026 survey shows agentic AI rising as a top priority from 13.0 to 17.1 percent year over year. Direction is up and to the right; coordinated autonomy is still rare.

Scenarios as a holdout set

The cleanest new technique comes from StrongDM's Attractor dark factory: three engineers, two non-negotiable rules (no human writes code, no human reviews code), and roughly 16,000 lines of Rust, 9,500 of Go, and 6,700 of TypeScript shipped since July 2025. Its design answers the central dark-factory question: how do you trust output no human reviews?

  • Scenarios, not tests. Traditional tests live inside the codebase, where the agent can see and game them, over-fitting to the test rather than the intent. StrongDM moves the standard of truth outside the codebase: a scenario is an end-to-end user story in natural language, stored apart, validated by an LLM, and hidden from the building agent during development.
  • Holdout-set correctness. Borrowing straight from machine learning, correctness is inferred only from externally observable behavior on scenarios the builder cannot access. Separate the generator from the standard of truth and over-fitting collapses.
  • Behavioral replicas of dependencies. The team built high-fidelity fakes of Okta, Jira, Slack, and Google Docs, Drive, and Sheets so agents can test against realistic edge cases at volume, without rate limits or production risk. A reusable pattern for any factory whose product talks to third-party APIs.

On the spec front, GitHub Spec Kit is now past 90,000 stars with more than 30 agent integrations, and the spec-driven command surface (including a read-only consistency gate that checks spec, plan, and tasks before any code) is stable. No structural change this cycle, which itself signals the front-end spec layer has settled. The active frontier has moved downstream to the verification and holdout layer, exactly where StrongDM is pushing.

The LinearB result is a 39-point gap between perception and measurement: more throughput into the queue, slower flow out of it. Sonar's "verification gap" means the gate is both distrusted and skipped. The operating lesson is now well evidenced: the loop is only as fast as its automated, independent verification. Bolt generation onto a manual review gate and you manufacture a slower team.

Make the gate independent of the builder, deterministic for security and policy pass-fail, and behavioral for intent. Score trajectories, not just the final diff.

Other proof points this cycle

  • StrongDM Attractor: about 32,000 lines of production code in Rust, Go, and TypeScript built and tested with no human authoring or review over roughly 11 months, governed by markdown specs plus external scenarios. The strongest mechanism proof point of the quarter, alongside Salesforce's velocity proof point.
  • Google Antigravity: an autonomous platform deploying agents that plan, execute, and verify across editor, terminal, and browser, consistent with Google's standing 75 percent AI-generated (suggested) code figure. The perennial gap remains between "75 percent suggested" and DX's roughly 22 percent merged: the delegation gap, quantified.
  • Belitsoft and Futurum: 12 agents per company on average, about half working alone, and agentic AI priority rising from 13.0 to 17.1 percent year over year.

Prompt injection, reframed as permanent

The security story hardened from incident to structural verdict. OWASP's GenAI Security Project published its State of Agentic AI Security and Governance v2.01 on June 11, 2026, arguing that prompt injection may not be a patchable bug at all: it is architectural, because an LLM has no built-in way to separate trusted commands from untrusted data when both arrive as the same token stream. Prompt injection maps to six of the ten categories in OWASP's Top 10 for Agentic Applications. The repositories with the most security advisories are telling for anyone wiring agents into CI: n8n (57), Claude Code (22), AutoGPT (15), Dify (13), and Roo-Code (11).

A Cloud Security Alliance research note on the Claude Code GitHub Action attack chain underlines how little novelty an attacker needs: the disclosed attack composed an authorization bypass, an indirect prompt injection, and environment-variable exfiltration, starting from an opened public GitHub issue and ending with malicious code pushed to the action's own repository.

IMPLICATION

If injection is a permanent property, the only durable defenses are containment, not cure: least-privilege tokens, no secrets in agent-reachable environments, human approval on irreversible verbs, and an independent gate the builder agent cannot influence. Plan for the agent to be occasionally adversarial by accident.

How we apply this at River.io

River.io's posture (native compiled code, zero third-party dependencies, test-driven development, GitHub-issue-driven SDLC) lines up unusually well with where the credible part of the field, as opposed to the hype, is heading.

The scenario holdout pattern is TDD with a firewall. StrongDM's move maps directly onto our testing discipline: unit tests stay in-repo for the build loop, and an out-of-repo scenario set (plain-language end-to-end behaviors, independently validated) is kept where the building agent never sees it. It is the cleanest way to keep an autonomous loop honest without a human reviewer, and it costs nothing in dependencies: scenarios are markdown plus a small native validator. We are proving this out on low-stakes repositories (MeowPassword, weathergalactic, sloth) by defining end-to-end behaviors as scenarios stored outside the source tree, with a tiny native validator that runs them against a built binary and reports pass or fail, no third-party test framework required.

Zero third-party dependencies is a security asset, not an aesthetic. This cycle's risk material, from OWASP's "permanent flaw" verdict to slopsquatting and hallucinated-dependency vectors, all rides the dependency supply chain. A zero-dependency, compiled codebase removes the single largest attack surface, and an autonomous loop can preserve that by policy with a deterministic gate: the build fails if any import is outside the standard library.

The issue-ingestion path gets hardened before any autonomy. The Claude Code GitHub Action attack chain begins with an opened public issue, and our SDLC is issue-driven. We treat all issue, PR, and comment bodies as untrusted data, never as instructions, and any agent that reads them runs with least-privilege tokens and no secrets in its environment. That guardrail holds regardless of how far we push autonomy.

Regulated work stays human-gated. CareTime and TimeForCare (Medicaid electronic visit verification, protected health information) and MaterialsAndPractices (organic-farm compliance) run at assisted autonomy with a human approval gate on every irreversible action. Anthropic's own 0-to-20-percent-delegable finding and LinearB's review-bottleneck data both say the same thing: full autonomy pays off only in narrow, well-specified, low-blast-radius domains, and regulated work is the opposite of that. Lights-out experiments are reserved for the repositories where the cost of failure is throwaway.
