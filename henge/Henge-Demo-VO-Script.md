# Henge: App Demo Voiceover Master Script

**river.io LLC · v1 · 27 August 2026**

Narration is in blockquotes. Everything else is direction and does not get read.

---

## How to use this script

This is a **modular master**. Record it once, straight through, in beat order with clean handles (two seconds of room tone) at every beat boundary. That single session yields five deliverables:

### Read direction

The app's own governing line is *"druidic in spirit and ceremony, archaeological in fact."* The read should sit in the same place. Not a trailer voice. Not awed. Closer to a good documentary narrator who genuinely knows the material and is not trying to sell you anything: unhurried, warm, specific, and completely unembarrassed about numbers.

Where a number appears, land on it. The numbers are the point. Do not throw them away as filler.

Pace target is 140 to 150 words per minute. Slower than a promo, faster than a meditation.

## BEAT 1 · Cold open

**Screen.** Black, two seconds. The Henge mark on pre-dawn slate. Cut to the Altar Stone camera station, eye height, facing northeast up the axis. Horizon still dark. Hold. Let the sun break beside the Heel Stone in real time, no speed-up.

> Four and a half thousand years ago, someone stood here and waited for this.
>
> Not a sunrise. This sunrise. The one that comes up the axis.
>
> The stones are still keeping the appointment.
>
> This is Henge. A photoreal Stonehenge that is also a working astronomical calendar. Stand where the builders stood, on any morning in five thousand years, and watch the light arrive.

**Short variant for the 30-second App Store preview** (record both):

> Four and a half thousand years ago, someone stood here and waited for this sunrise. The one that comes up the axis. The stones are still keeping the appointment. Henge. A photoreal Stonehenge that is also a working astronomical calendar. Stand where the builders stood and watch the light arrive.

## BEAT 2 · The claim

**Screen.** Pull back off the Altar Stone. Scrub the time control forward at moderate speed so the sun climbs and the shadows sweep. Cut to the readout showing the live solar bearing.

> Start with the thing that makes the rest of it worth your attention.
>
> The sun in Henge is not an animation. Nobody keyframed that arc. It is computed, from the same ephemeris astronomers use, and it holds its accuracy across five thousand years. Solar longitude inside a thousandth of a degree.
>
> The tilt of the Earth's axis is solved for each epoch, because in 2500 BC it was not what it is today. Even the slow wobble in the length of the day is accounted for, across five thousand years of it, which is the correction that separates an app that is roughly right about the Bronze Age from one that is actually right.
>
> Any app that pretends otherwise is drawing a picture. It is not keeping time.

---

## BEAT 3 · The shadows

**Screen.** Overhead. A single trilithon, shadow long across the grass. Optionally overlay the analytic centre line as a thin bronze rule for two seconds, then drop it.

> Which brings us to the shadows.
>
> In a normal three-dimensional scene, shadows are decoration. In Henge they are the readout. The whole claim of the app rests on them, so the app was built to be caught if it lies.
>
> The geometry module solves each shadow on paper, with no graphics involved at all. The renderer draws it in Metal. And then a test walks out along the shadow's centre line in an overhead render and measures where the drawn edge actually falls. It has to agree with the solved answer inside twenty-eight centimetres on the ground.
>
> That number was measured, not chosen. If a change to the renderer moves it, the number gets re-measured and re-justified. It does not get relaxed.

---

## BEAT 4 · The monument

**Screen.** Slow orbit at eye height through the sarsen ring, then rising to the aerial station. Bring up the Petrie numbers briefly as the camera passes a stone. Cut to a low angle showing the ridge line on the horizon.

> The monument you are standing in is the whole thing.
>
> Thirty sarsen uprights, and the continuous lintel ring riding on top of them. The five great trilithons of the inner horseshoe. The bluestone circle and the bluestone horseshoe inside that. The Altar Stone. The Slaughter Stone and its lost portal companion. The Heel Stone, out past the ditch. The four Station Stones in their rectangle, and the fifty-six Aubrey holes ringing all of it.
>
> Every stone carries its Petrie number, the same numbering archaeologists have used since the 1877 survey, so what you are looking at can be checked against the literature.
>
> Get close and the surfaces hold up. Weathered sarsen and Preseli bluestone, each with its own grain. Damp rising in the stone at the foot where it meets the soil. Lichen. Wind moving through the grass, which is modelled at the height grass actually grows, because at true physical scale a mistake in a blade of grass turns the plain into a jungle and every test still passes.
>
> And it is standing on real ground. The terrain under your feet is Salisbury Plain's own survey data. The ridge the midsummer sun clears is the ridge that is actually there. That matters more than it sounds. A sunrise over a flat mathematical horizon sits a full degree away from a sunrise over real land, and one degree, at Stonehenge, is the difference between an alignment and a coincidence.

---

## BEAT 5 · Two states, and nothing in between

**Screen.** Standing in the ruin. Hit the monument state toggle. Let the transition play at full length: the bank eroding backwards, the stones arriving in construction order, the ring closing. Land on "as it was."

> Now watch this.
>
> Henge holds two states of the monument, and only two. As it stands today, weathered and fallen and gapped. And as it was, whole, when the ring was closed.
>
> There is no slider between them. You cannot park the monument at sixty percent ruined, because a sixty percent ruined Stonehenge is not something anyone has evidence for, and the app refuses to invent one. Two labelled states, each traceable to survey data.
>
> What you get in between is the passage itself. Flip the toggle and the earthwork erodes, the stones arrive in construction order, the bank rises and settles. A few seconds of it, and then it lands cleanly on the other state. It is presentation, not a claim.
>
> And if you have Reduce Motion switched on, it skips the whole performance and simply changes.

## BEAT 6 · The Wheel of the Year

**Screen.** Open the year bar. Show the season strip and the eight festival lights. Tap Samhain. Let the app land on sunrise. Show the tier badge appearing next to the festival name.

> Open the year bar and the calendar comes forward.
>
> The Wheel of the Year runs along the bottom. The two solstices, the two equinoxes, and the four cross-quarter festivals. Imbolc, Beltane, Lughnasadh, Samhain.
>
> Each of those eight is solved from where the sun actually is, not looked up from a table of dates. And tap one and the app does not jump to midnight on a calendar day. It jumps to the sunrise of that day, because sunrise is when the thing you came to see happens.
>
> Here is a small honest number the app does not hide. The customary cross-quarter dates, the first of February, the first of May, run three to seven days ahead of where the sun genuinely reaches those points. The Wheel shows you both. It would have been easy to quietly move the sun. Henge moves the label instead.

---

## BEAT 7 · The moon

**Screen.** Night. Moon low and gibbous over the trilithons. Push in on the lunar disc so the surface detail and the earthshine on the dark limb are unmistakable. Cut to the events ribbon showing an approaching standstill.

> The moon is solved too, and it is the harder problem.
>
> Position, phase, distance and apparent size, from a truncated ELP-2000 series, corrected for where you are standing on the surface of the Earth rather than at its centre. The face is the real one, photographed by NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter. And when it is a crescent, the dark part is not black. That is earthshine, and it is lit the way earthshine is lit.
>
> The eighteen-point-six-year nodal cycle is in there. So are the eclipse seasons, and the lunar standstills, major and minor, where the moon's rising point swings out past anything the sun ever does. None of that is scripted. It emerges from the arithmetic, which is the only way you would ever trust it.
>
> And when the moon is up and bright enough, it casts shadows of its own.
>
> There is one more thing here worth your time. There is an old idea, argued over since the 1960s, that the fifty-six Aubrey holes were an eclipse counter. Move markers around the ring, one hole at a time, and the ring predicts eclipses. Henge will run it for you, badged as hypothesis, and then it does the thing an app almost never does. It scores the hypothesis against the real ephemeris and shows you the result. Over a decade: forty-two eclipses caught, none missed, nine false alarms. Make of that what you will. The app is not going to make of it for you.

## BEAT 8 · The sky over the builders

**Screen.** Look up. Full star field. Bring in the constellation figures. Scrub the date backwards several thousand years and hold on the pole so the viewer can see Polaris drift off it and Thuban take its place. Then the zodiac toggle.

> Then there is the sky above all of it.
>
> The naked-eye stars come from the Hipparcos catalogue, carried with proper motion and precession. Which means the sky over the builders is not the sky over you.
>
> Wind the date back to 2800 BC and Polaris drifts off the pole, useless to anybody, and Thuban, in Draco, takes its place. Nobody hardcoded that. It falls out of the precession mathematics, which is exactly the sort of thing you want happening on its own before you believe anything else the app tells you.
>
> Five naked-eye planets ride the same sky, sitting steady while the stars shimmer, the way they really do. Twelve zodiac constellations behind their own toggle, precessing along with everything else.
>
> And the constellation figures, the joining lines, are drawn by hand. Twenty-nine of them. One hundred and eighty-eight segments. The licensing on published constellation art was a wall, so rather than fudge it or drop the feature, the lines were authored from scratch against the catalogue. The membership is Ptolemy's. The positions are Hipparcos's. The drawing is ours.

## BEAT 9 · Five thousand years, one scrubber

**Screen.** Aerial. Run the time-lapse up hard so the shadows spin. Then stop dead on a solstice sunrise. Then the date-travel picker: type 2500 BC, land, show the bearing readout change.

> All of it runs on one scrubber.
>
> Speed it up and the shadows sweep the ground like a hand across a dial, which is what they always were. Slow it down and hold a single minute of a solstice sunrise for as long as you like.
>
> Or jump. Any date from 3000 BC to AD 3000. Five thousand years of sky, in both directions, and the alignments shift as you travel, because the tilt of the Earth is moving underneath them. The midsummer sunrise bearing in 2500 BC is not the midsummer sunrise bearing today, and Henge shows you the drift rather than flattening it.
>
> You can also put the sky over your own longitude instead of over Wiltshire, and watch your own solstice arrive.

## BEAT 10 · Honest archaeology

**Screen.** Open a lore panel. Hold long enough to read the tier badge and the citation. Cut to the ground-plan overlay with the axis line drawn and the bearing labelled.

> Which brings us to the part of Henge that matters most.
>
> Every claim the app makes about the monument carries a tier. Established. Debated. Or modern tradition. And at least one citation, one tap away, at the point where you read the claim rather than buried in a credits screen.
>
> The solstice axis is established. The equinoxes at Stonehenge are modern tradition, and Henge says so plainly instead of letting you assume otherwise.
>
> Here is what that honesty costs, and why it is worth paying. The built axis of the monument surveys at forty-nine point nine degrees. When Henge computes the midsummer sunrise for 2500 BC over the real skyline, it gets forty-nine point zero eight. Eight tenths of a degree off. But the midwinter sunset, the opposite direction along the very same line, lands two tenths off.
>
> Midwinter fits better. That is a real result, straight out of the ephemeris. It is a genuinely interesting one. And a lesser app would have quietly nudged the sun until the tidier answer came out. Henge gives you the number, and then explains the argument.

---

## BEAT 11 · How it is built

**Screen.** Golden hour. Slow move through the ring with light shafts breaking between the stones. Then a quiet beat on the VoiceOver focus ring reading a stone label.

> A word about how it is built, because it shows.
>
> There is no game engine under this. No SceneKit, no RealityKit, no Unity. It is hand-written Metal 3. A physically modelled sky. Physically based materials for weathered sarsen and Preseli bluestone. Three-cascade shadow maps, with the softness of the shadow edge derived from the sun's real angular size. Film-grade tone mapping on the way out.
>
> There are no third-party dependencies at all. And there is no network. No account, no sign-in, no telemetry, no analytics. Nothing collected, nothing sent. The almanac works in a field with no signal, which is where an almanac ought to work.
>
> Every stone and every control carries a VoiceOver label. Dynamic Type runs through the chrome. Reduce Motion gets a real alternative rather than a disabled feature.

---

## BEAT 12 · The hours nobody lists

**Screen.** Rapid but unhurried sequence: rain on the plain, then frost, then the individual stone surfaces close up, then the ground-plan overlay, then hard cut to torchlight at night.

> And then there are the hours nobody puts in a feature list.
>
> Golden-hour light shafts, marched against the same shadow cascades that carry the calendar, so the beams break exactly where the stones block them.
>
> Weather you choose. Overcast, rain, frost on the grass.
>
> Every stone individually generated, so no two are twins, the way no two sarsens are.
>
> A ground-plan overlay with the cardinals, the construction lines and the surveyed measurements laid onto the terrain rather than floating above it.
>
> Four fixed camera stations at eye height, one metre seventy, because that is where a person's eyes are and the alignments were built to be seen from there. Drag to look. Pinch to change what you can take in at once, the way you would lean forward.
>
> And after dark, torchlight. Ceremony mode, flickering on wall-clock time, the fires throwing the stones' shadows outward across the plain.

## Recording notes

1. **Record beat by beat, not in one take.** Every beat boundary is a cut point in at least one deliverable. Two seconds of silence between beats gives the editor room.
2. **Beat 1 twice.** Full version and the short App Store variant. They are not interchangeable.
3. **Beat 13 twice.** Full close and the hero close.
4. **Numbers get a second pass.** Record beats 3, 6, 10 and 13 a second time slightly slower. Those four carry the figures the whole pitch rests on, and you will want the option in the edit.
5. **The music, if any, should stop under beat 10.** Let the honesty beat play dry. It is the most persuasive thing in the script and it will be more persuasive without a bed.
6. **What not to say**, ever, in any cut: "immersive experience," "unlock the mysteries," "ancient secrets," "like never before." The entire positioning is that Henge is the one Stonehenge app that does not talk like that.
